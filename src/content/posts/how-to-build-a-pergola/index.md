How in the world did I decide it was a good idea to DIY a pergola in my backyard? I’ll tell you how: Instagram Reels. Reels of people (probably professionals) paving patios and raising entire structures, all squeezed into a sub-minute montage. An hour of scrolling later, I had a full plan, an itemized supply list, and zero doubt that I could build a patio *and* a pergola myself. Then I started digging…

Keep in mind that this whole project took place smack in the middle of a Texas summer, when it hit a blistering 100 degrees by 10 a.m. every day. Did that stop me? No. No, it did not. So today I’m walking you through how I built my pergola, so you’ll know what not to do if you’re crazy enough to try it yourself.

**Step 1:** Don’t decide to build a pergola in the middle of a Texas summer.  
**Step 2:** Dig, dig, dig.

## Decide on the size

Okay, but in all seriousness: start by deciding the dimensions of the patio you’ll build your pergola on. A good guide is what you want to put underneath it. I knew I wanted a six-person outdoor dining table, so a 12x14 ft pergola would comfortably fit the average-size table I had in mind. With the pergola’s size settled, I added 1 ft on each side, making the whole patio 14x16 ft.

## Map out the patio

Drawing is going to be your best friend when it comes to deciding where to put your patio. You can redo a drawing a million times, but you can’t move a patio. Measure the space: your yard, the fence line, the house, the distance from one tree to another. Give yourself some kind of measurement to work from in your drawing, ideally both length and width.

Next, I spray-painted an outline of the patio on the grass. This sounds easy. It’s not. That’s why the drawings are your practice round. On paper, I thought I knew exactly where my pergola would go, but once I got out there, the position of my house’s window sent me in a completely different direction. I ended up respraying the outline three times. Ideally, you won’t change your mind, but if you do, that’s the beauty of spray paint: like the drawings, it isn’t permanent.

## Till and dig

Now we’re entering more permanent territory: digging. If you live in Texas like I do, I recommend renting a tiller and tilling the area before you dig (especially if your patio is as big as mine). It breaks up the hard soil and makes digging a lot easier. Luckily, my dad was around with his truck to help me rent the tiller, and he did the tilling himself.

I thought my dad was going to take his legs out with that Home Depot tiller. The machine had a ton of unexpected power and was genuinely dangerous. I still recommend using one, but with extreme caution (or get your dad to do it).

After tilling, my dad and I spent about a week and a half digging. By 10 a.m. it was too hot to dig, so we were out there from 7 to 10 every morning. I decided about three inches deep left enough room for sand, paver base, and pavers and would still let the patio sit flush with the grass. Luckily, that depth worked out (otherwise, we’d have had to dig more). Afterward, I used a hand tamper to pack down and level the ground. Finally, I laid down the weed barrier. One tip: get good gloves for this whole process. Mine were about three sizes too big, so I was constantly dealing with blisters.

## Pour the concrete footings

Next, I had to figure out how to anchor the pergola to the ground. This is a must, especially in Texas. I had two options: (1) bury the posts in holes and fill them with concrete, or (2) pour concrete footings and drill the posts into them. I went with the second. So even after I thought I was done digging, I had four more foot-deep holes to dig.

You might think four holes would be easy, but these holes were permanent, so I wanted to get them right the first time. The last thing I wanted was to find out later that they were a few inches off from the pergola’s dimensions. So my dad and I assembled the first part of the pergola and used it to measure exactly where the holes should go. (Note: I recommend having at least three people to build the pergola and stand it up. With just the two of us, it was extremely difficult.)

Every Home Depot near us was out of the concrete form tubes we needed, so we used Home Depot buckets instead, and they worked perfectly. To let the concrete meet the ground at the bottom of each hole and create a solid anchor, I cut the bottoms out of the buckets, which made them pretty close to a real concrete form. Then I set the buckets in the holes and made sure they were level with each other, using a piece of plywood long enough to span the holes. Don’t skip the leveling unless you want a crooked pergola. Finally, I filled the buckets with Quikrete. (This part was actually very satisfying. I highly recommend playing with concrete at some point in your life.)

## Sand and paver base

After all that digging, I thought things would get easier. They didn’t. Next up was sand. I bought it wholesale and had it delivered; they dropped it in my driveway, and I shoveled it into a wheelbarrow and hauled it out back. The hard part was the patience it took to level a 14x16 ft space. In the middle of summer, the last thing you want is to be bent over on your hands and knees in the scorching sun, methodically leveling sand. My mistake at first was trying to do it all at once. Things got easier when I split the space into 4x4 ft sections and leveled them one at a time. It was mentally and physically painful, and I was elated when it was done.

Instead of buying a ton of rock and having to shovel and level it, I opted for the easier solution: paver base panels. Ask me again in ten years whether this was a good idea. At the time, though, it worked out nicely. The panels were easy to cut to size with a box cutter, and this ended up being one of the easiest steps of the whole project. I highly recommend them.

## Lay the pavers

Lowe’s conveniently delivered the pavers and set them inside my garage. My friend Sophia and I then hauled all 250 of them to the backyard, and I started laying them. At first, I brute-forced it. Unfortunately, the design I wanted (straight diagonal lines) actually needed some planning behind it. About halfway through, I was so proud of my work—until I looked at reference pictures. I was completely off, with diagonals at all kinds of wrong angles. I had to start over. This time, I used a mason’s line to mark guide lines across the patio so I wouldn’t have to start over a third time.

## Build the pergola

Finally, the patio was done! Next up: the pergola. Luckily, I had great friends to help. The pergola was a kit from Home Depot, so with a lot of drilling, instruction-following, and teamwork, the three of us got the job done.

## Finishing touches

When I laid the pavers, I left a square gap around each pergola post so the concrete footings weren’t covered. To finish, I filled those squares around the posts with pebble gravel. Then I spread polymeric sand over the pavers to fill the gaps between them.

## Was it worth it?

So, was it worth it? A week and a half of digging, more blisters than I can count, and three rounds of spray paint later, I have a patio and a pergola in my backyard that I built with my own two hands (plus a few extra pairs). Now, every time I sit under it at my six-person dining table, I forget all about those 100-degree mornings. Almost. Would I do it again? Yes. Would I do it again in the middle of a Texas summer? No. No, I would not.

Before I go, I want to thank my dad and my friends Sophia and Dylan for helping me through this process. I was crazy enough to think I could do it all myself, but looking back, I can’t imagine pulling it off without them. They kept me company through the torture (digging, tilling, drilling), and my heart is so full knowing they were right there with me. You guys are the best.
